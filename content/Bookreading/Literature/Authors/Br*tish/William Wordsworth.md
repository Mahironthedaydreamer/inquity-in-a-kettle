## I. Background

- William Wordsworth (1770-1850) was born in Cockermount, Cumberland, in the Lake district

- His father- John Wordsworth, was the attorney of Sir James Lowther- the fifth Baronet Lowther (the most feared and hated aristocrat in all of Cumberland and Westmoreland) => An Intolerable Tyrant over his Tenants and Dependents"

- He lost his mother when he was eight, and his father when he was 13. Domestic problems separated him from his beloved and neurotic sister Dorothy- a very important person in his life

- He was raised amid the mountains of Cumberland alongside the river Derwent. Wordsworth grew up in a rustic society, and spent a great deal of his time playing outdoors( a pure communion with nature)  
  \=> William Wordsworth’s love for nature and imagination stemmed from the magnificent landscape of the rugged and lovely land of stream, hill, cataracts, and precipitous mountains

- Early 1790s: he lived for a time in France, and in this time had an affair with Annette Vallon, with whom he had a daughter (Caroline)

- A later journey to France to meet Caroline, now a young girl, would inspire the great sonnet “It is a beauteous evening, calm and free”

- Freed from financial worries by a legacy left to him in 1795, Wordsworth moved to Racedown with his sister, and then to Alfoxden (Grasmere), where he would be closer with his friend and fellow poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge. They began work on a book called Lyrical Ballads, first published in 1798, then reissued with Wordsworth monumental preface in 1802

- After “Lyrical Ballads” and “ The Prelude”, a massive autobiography in verse form, Wordsworth moved the stately house at Rydal Mount where he lived with Dorothy, his wife Mary, and his children until his death in 1850

## II. Works 

### 1. Lyrical Ballads:

- The publication of Lyrical Ballads represents a landmark moment for English poetry; it was unlike anything that had come before, and paved the way for everything that has come after

- According to the theory that poetry resulted from the “spontaneous overflow” of emotions, as Wordsworth wrote in the preface, Wordsworth and Coleridge made it their task to write in the simple language of common people, telling stories of their life.

- The theory that poetry resulted from the “spontaneous overflow” of emotions: poetry originated in “emotion recollected in a state of tranquillity”; the poet then surrendered to the emotion, so that the tranquillity dissolved, and the emotion remained in the poem.
  \=> This explicit emphasis on feeling, simplicity and the pleasure of beauty over rhetoric, ornament and formality changed the course of English poetry, replacing the elaborate classical forms of Pope and Dryden with a new Romantic sensibility 

- Wordsworth’s vision of nature: 
  - Wordsworth is perhaps not good at describing the natural landscape as a number of other poets. As a purely descriptive poet he is highly capable, but his genius lies in showing what happens when the innate power of nature meets the power of perception of human mind
  - It is as if the individual’s perception of nature, its awe, and capacity to teach, is what matters, rather than nature itself. It is the interaction of nature and human nature that enlivens and stimulates Wordsworth.
  - The word pantheism is sometimes used in connections with Wordsworth. (a pantheist believes that God is visible in everything, and that his nature can be ascertained from the nature of his creation, the universe) 
    \=> In a restricted sense, Wordsworth is a Pantheist

- Wordsworth’s opinions of poetry: 
  - Poetry should be written in the natural language of common speech, rather than in the lofty and elaborate dictions that were considered “poetic”
  - Poetry should offer access to the emotions contained in memory 
  - The first principle of poetry should be pleasure, that the chief duty is to provide pleasure through a rhythmic and beautiful expression of feeling- for all human sympathy, he claims, is based on a subtle pleasure principle that is “the naked and native dignity of man”

- Wordsworth’s most important legacy, besides his lovely, timeless poems, is his launching of the Romantic era, opening the gates for later writers such as John Keats, Percy Bysshe Shelly, and Lord Byron in England, Thoreau in America.

## I. Background

- The Moon and Sixpence (1919) is a novel by William Somerset Maugham.
- It was inspired by the life of the French painter Paul Gauguin, who abandoned his family and career to pursue art.
- The work reflects the conflict between material life and individual creative aspiration.
- The title “The Moon and Sixpence”:
  - Moon: high ideals, art, dreams
  - Sixpence: money, security, ordinary daily life
- → Conflict: art vs society

## II. Summary

- Main character: Charles Strickland, a successful London stockbroker who suddenly gives up everything to become an artist. “I‘ve got to paint”
- In Paris, Strickland lives in poverty but dedicates himself completely to art. He befriends Dirk Stroeve, a Dutch painter who recognizes his talent and tries to help him. But tragedy strikes: Dirk’s wife, Blanche, falls in love with Strickland, gets rejected, and commits suicide.
- Later, Strickland moves to Tahiti, marries a local woman, and lives happily for a while. Stricken with leprosy, he creates a final masterpiece, then asks his wife to burn it to prevent commercialization.

## III. Message

- The novel criticizes materialistic society obsessed with money, status, and hypocrisy.
- It affirms the power of art and the necessity of creative freedom, even at the cost of suffering and isolation.
- “Are we living only for comfort, or are we brave enough to follow our passion?”

## IV. Chapter XII

### 1. Structure

- A dialogue-driven narrative.
- Around conversation; the narrator - Strickland
  ⇒ The main vehicle for characterization and conflict.

### 2. Plot summary

- Strickland abandons his wife and two children to become a painter
- He shows no guilt or emotion -  only obsession with art.
  → a callous and selfish individual.

### 3. Theme

- The conflict between artistic passion and societal responsibility:
- Strickland's actions embody this theme directly, as he sacrifices his family and reputation to pursue a life as a painter.
- The nature of the genius:
  - Portraying Strickland as a genius driven by an almost monstrous obsession and detached from normal human emotions and morality.
- Questions about true happiness and moral sacrifice:
  - Whether one can find "happiness or contentment" through such a selfish and destructive path.

### 4. Character

- Strickland:
  - A highly unconventional and rather unpleasant individual
  - Dismissive, brutal, and completely self-absorbed.
  - Has an intense and unwavering desire
    → Strickland is a dynamic character
- The Narrator:
  - A friend of the family, who interacts with him.
  - Moral, shocked but slowly fascinated.
  - Calling Strickland "a most unmitigated cad"
  - Commenting on his "brutal" and "abominable" behavior.

### 5. Setting

- Paris, Avenue de Clichy
  - Lively and vibrant but sordid and crowded
  - Symbolic of freedom
- Cheap Hotel Room
  - Small, shabby, poverty-stricken.
  - Reflects Strickland’s rejection of comfort

### 6. Point of view

- First-person narrator
  - Creates bias, moral commentary, and shifting tones.
  - Increases relatability, but also exposes the narrator’s limited perspective
- Development of Understanding
  - Moves from disbelief and judgment to fascination and horror.
  - Uses irony against himself, forcing readers to weigh conventional morality against Strickland’s uncompromising vision.

### 7. Language and Style

#### a, Metaphor

- The drowning man
  - “When a man falls into the water it doesn’t matter how he swims, well or badly: he’s got to get out or else he’ll drown.”
- Possession of a demon
- “He seemed really to be possessed of a devil, and I felt that it might suddenly turn and rend him.”

#### b, Imagery

- Physical description of Strickland
  - “His trousers were baggy, his hands were not clean; and his face, with the red stubble of the unshaved chin, the little eyes, and the large, aggressive
    nose, as uncouth and coarse.”
- Setting imagery
  - “The Avenue de Clichy was crowded... clerks and shopgirls; old fellows who might have stepped out of the pages of Honore de Balzac.”
    → Society’s sordid and forgotten lives

### c, Repetition

- Obsessive Mantra
  - Repetition of “I’ve got to paint” (4x) emphasizes compulsion.
  - Suggests art is not a choice but a destiny or addiction.
- The Effect of Repetition
  - “It gave me the sensation of something very strong, overmastering, that held him, as it were, against his will.”
    → Demonic obsession

### d, Symbolism

- Appearance vs Essence: His shabby, dirty body symbolizes his rejection of materialism and respectability.
- The Moon and Sixpence: “If you look on the ground in search of a sixpence, you don’t look up, and so miss the moon”
  ⇒ Chapter XII dramatizes Strickland’s decisive rejection of sixpence for the moon.

### e, Irony

- Situational Irony: Society suspects adultery - truth is worse: he left for art.
- Dramatic irony: Narrator cannot see that Strickland’s “madness” is genius; the reader is meant to sense it.
- Verbal Irony: Strickland’s curt replies mock the narrator’s seriousness.

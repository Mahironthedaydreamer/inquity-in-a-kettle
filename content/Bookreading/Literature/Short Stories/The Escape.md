## I. Overview

- Year of publication: 1926, in Somerset Maugham’s collection of short stories
- Content of the story:
  - In early 20th-century England, marriage was shaped more by status and wealth than by love.
  - At that time, marriage was seen as a “social duty”, especially for women
  - Maugham, with his characteristic satirical style, reflected and mocked this very reality in the story.
- Genre: a short story with a strong satirical and ironic tone.
- Satire: Criticizes materialism in love and the social constraints of marriage.
- Irony: Roger escapes the relationship not by direct refusal but through a tiring strategy - taking Ruth to see countless houses until she herself gives up.

## II. Plot summary

- The plot of the story is quite simple, though interesting.
- The protagonist of the story, a young man rolling in money - Roger Charing - fell in love with Ruth Barlow, an unfortunate woman who was twice a widow.
- They had all the happy and pleasant moments of relationships a loving couple usually has and intended to marry.
- Then suddenly Roger fell out of love with Ruth. But he found a way to make Ruth release him.
- He said they would marry the day they found the perfect house for both of them; however, Roger rejected all the offers of the agents offering a new house.
- At last, Ruth lost her patience and left Roger herself.
  → The story explores the theme of relationships between men and women, but also touches upon the social problem of planned marriages, the general insincerity that exists between men and women (particularly in marital affairs), and with the problem of people's dependence upon the public opinion.

## III. Characters

- Roger Charing:
  - A tall, handsome, rich, experienced middle-aged man.
  - Many women want to marry him, but he is happy to live the life of an unmarried man
- Ruth Barlow:
  - A quite good-looking woman with big splendid dark eyes full of sadness (she is twice a widow).
  - Initially portrayed as a helpless victim
  - According to the narrator’s opinion, Ruth was a two-faced woman of few ideas: he called her stupid and scheming
  - Is revealed to be a clever, self-interested "husband-hunter"
- Narrator
  - A detached, cynical friend of Roger
  - Observes and analyzes the events, serving as Maugham's voice of irony and social commentary.
  - The narration constantly switches from the third person, when the author simply tells the story of the characters, to first person, as the author participates in the plot himself as a friend of the personages.
  - There are also dialogues, both between Roger and the author and between Roger and Ruth.
    → It increases the credibility of the plot

## IV. Themes

- Escape:
  - Marriage is a "trap"  which must be cleverly escaped
  - It is not based on genuine love.
- Hypocrisy:
  - Upper-class obsession with appearances.
  - Prioritize reputation and social etiquette over honesty and genuine feelings.
- Cynical View of Marriage:
  - Marriage as a transactional arrangement & motivated by financial gain rather than love.

## V. Literary Style and Devices

- Ironic Tone: The narrator's detached and cynical voice ⟶  a strong sense of irony
- Metaphor: "House-hunting" for "escape.
- First-person narrative: An observing narrator allows Maugham to offer sharp, subtle social commentary without directly stating his opinions.

## VI. Critical Analysis

- Shows how a man avoids marrying a woman who tries to trap him.
- View of Marriage
  - Freedom vs. Security
  - Trap / Stability
- Irony & Humor
  - Cowardice as Kindness
  - Comic House-Hunting
- Character Portrayal
  - Clever but Manipulative
  - Fragile yet Calculating
- Social Criticism
  - Reputation > Sincerity
  - Marriage as Transaction

## VII. Conclusion

- The story teaches us that marriage is not always about love—it can also be about need, freedom, and social pressure.
- The man’s “escape” is more than just leaving a woman; it is also escaping from society’s expectations.

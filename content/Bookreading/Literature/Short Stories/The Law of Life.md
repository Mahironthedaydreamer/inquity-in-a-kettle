## I. Type

- Genre: Naturalism
- Power of Nature is absolute and individual is insignificant before natural laws
- Tone and Style: simple, descriptive and objective
- Theme: the Inevitability of Death and the Cycle of Life

## II. Background

- Year of publication: First published in 1901 in
- McClure’s Magazine, later included in the collection The Children of the Frost (1902).
- Setting: Arctic Wilderness
- Main characters: Old Koskoosh, tribe members and the wolves.

## III. The story’s timeline

### 1. Present: Abandonment and Acceptance

- Summary: Old Koskoosh sits alone beside a dying fire. His tribe has left him behind, following their tradition of abandoning the old and weak who can no longer contribute to survival. His son, the leader of the hunting party, has said his last goodbye, and Koskoosh does not resist.
- Main conflict:
  - The contrast between human emotion and nature’s brutal law.
  - Koskoosh does not beg for life → understanding of natural law - all creatures must die.
  - The fire - his remaining life, flickering and growing weaker.

### 2, Past: Reflections on life and survival

- Summary: As he waits for death, Koskoosh recalls different moments from his past.

#### a.His childhood and the cycle of life

- Koskoosh remembers when he was young and saw an old moose being hunted by wolves. The moose fought but could not escape, much like how Koskoosh now faces death.
  → His own situation - just as animals age and become prey, so do humans.
  → The story’s naturalistic philosophy, where survival is dictated by strength, not morality.

#### b. Memories of famine and harsh winters

- Koskoosh recalls times when his tribe faced starvation during long winters. The strong survived while the weak perished.
  →Idea: Nature does not favor anyone, even humans.
  → Highlight: the cruelty of natural selection, where only those who adapt can live.

#### c. The death of his mother and the loss of friends

- He also remembers personal losses - his mother and others who had been left behind before him. Koskoosh accepts his fate as part of the cycle.
  → Realization: he is no different from those who came before him; death is inevitable.

### 3, Present: The wolves and final acceptance

- Summary: As the fire continues to die, Koskoosh hears the approach of wolves. He understands that they are coming for him, just as they once hunted the old moose.
  → Considers fighting → recognizes the futility of resistance.
- Acceptance: The law of life
- Ending: His silent surrender as the wolves close in, mirroring the cycle he had seen throughout his life.

### 4, Writing technique

#### a, Third-person limited perspective and isolation

- Focus: the inner world of Old Koskoosh → allows the reader to experience his thoughts, memories, and emotions intimately.
- The limited view to Koskoosh’s consciousness → highlight his isolation as he faces death alone → quiet resignation to fate and acceptance of "the law of life".

#### b, Blending past and present (Circular structure)

Reinforces the theme: the cycle of life (the vibrancy of youth → the necessity of death)
Creates a symbolic closure: final act of defiance - the fate of the old moose → symbolically closes the loop on his life → reaffirms the unyielding law of nature

#### c, Concise and simple language

London does not use unnecessary details or fancy words. Instead, he expresses deep ideas with few words, making the story easy to understand yet powerful.

### 5. Literary devices

#### a, Symbolism

- The dying fire:
  - Represents Koskoosh’s remaining life - as it weakens, so does he.
  - When the fire finally dies out, it symbolizes the end of his existence.
  - This connects to the naturalist idea that life is fragile and fleeting.
    → The fire and life are directly linked - both fading away inevitably.

- The wolves
  - Symbolize the brutal laws of nature - they come to consume the weak - death claims all living things.
  - Their instinctual behavior reflects the tribe’s actions - leaving behind the old is not cruel, but necessary for survival.
    → He recognizes that they follow the same natural law as his people.

- The old moose
  - Stands for Koskoosh’s own fate - the moose once fought but ultimately succumbed, Koskoosh now faces the same unavoidable end.
    → This mirrors Koskoosh’s final moment, showing the universality of death.

#### b, Metaphor

> "I am as a last year's leaf, clinging lightly to the stem. The first breath that blows, and I fall."

- Koskoosh compares himself to a dead leaf still attached to a tree branch.
  → He is fragile, nearing the end of his life, and waiting for the inevitable moment when he "falls" (dies).
- The "first breath that blows" - the forces of nature or time that will ultimately take him away, reinforcing the natural cycle of life and death

> "He also was an episode, and would pass away."

- Life - a short episode, reducing individual existence to a mere moment.
  → The fleeting nature of human life
  → Reinforce the theme of mortality and the insignificance of the individual in the larger cycle of existence.

#### c, Personification

> “Nature was not kindly to the flesh. She had no concern for that concrete thing called the individual.”

- Nature personified as indifferent “she.”
- Humans are powerless against nature’s laws

> "The fire died down and the frost bit deeper."

- Fire = life; Frost = death.
  → Contrast of warmth vs. cold = cycle of life and death.

> "Death waited, ever-hungry and hungriest of them all."
> "Death came to him as it comes to all men."

- Death acts like a living predator — hungry and patient.
  → Reflects the inevitable fate and powerlessness of humans.

#### d, Onomatopoeia verbs

- Jack London uses sound words to create a realistic and immersive environment.
- They make the Arctic feel alive and reflect Koskoosh’s weakening senses
  - “Crackling” – sound of frozen hides, showing cold and tension
  - “Churned” – rough sled movement, showing hardship
  - “Snarled” – harsh sound of whips, showing cruelty
  - “Crunched” – footsteps on snow, making the scene real

#### e, Imagery

- Cold & Harsh Setting: “The snow fell lightly, settling on his shriveled hands.”
  → Highlights frailty, nature’s quiet reclaiming.
- Animal Imagery: “Even as the moose fell, the pack rushed upon it, their fangs tearing at the flesh”
  → Mirrors Koskoosh’s fate, survival of the fittest

#### f, Irony

> “He had seen it before. He had done it before. Now, it was his turn.”

- Highlights cycle of life & death
- Shows acceptance of natural law
- Reflects Naturalism & inevitability

#### g, Tone

- Serious & reflective
- Philosophical acceptance of death
- Respect for tradition & nature’s rules
- Cold, objective storytelling

#### h, Mood

- Somber and tragic: we feel the sadness of abandonment.
- Hopeless yet peaceful: Koskoosh accepts his fate calmly.
- Cold and tense: created by the harsh Arctic setting.
- Overall mood reinforces the inevitability of death.

#### i, Naturalism

- Nature controls human life
- The weak must give way to the strong
- Death = part of the survival cycle

#### l, Universal message

- All must face death
- Life goes on after the individual
- Acceptance brings peace

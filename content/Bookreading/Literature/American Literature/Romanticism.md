- Notable authors:
  - [[Emily Dickinson]]
- During 1700s-1800s: Dominant literary mode in Europe
- In reaction to Enlightenment + its emphasis on reason, Romanticism put its stress on:
  - Emotion
  - Imagination
  - Subjectivity of approach
- Influenced the major forms of American prose until ~1870.
  - Transcendentalism
    - Developed in New England as an American version of romanticism.
    - Rejected 18-century rationalism + established religion (Puritan tradition)
    - Celebrated the power of the human imagination -> Commune with the universe and transcend limitations of the material world.
    - Inspiration in nature
  - Historical Fiction: Cooper, Hawthorne and Others
    - Expression of romanticism in its probings of human nature/emotions + romanticizing of the American past and frontier
    - Popular subjects: First generations of Puritans, Salem witchcraft trials, white conflicts with Native Americans, American Revolution
  - Good and Evil
    - Can be inferred from Moby Dick - a "darkly metaphysical, symbolic and complex tale of Ahab in the pursuit of the white whale -  his embodiment of evil"
      - Mysterious forces of the universe that overwhelm the individual who seeks to confront + struggle against them.
    - Hawthorne and Melville demonstrated that asking questions about the nature of the universe could lead to answers illuminating the darker sides of life
      \- In the depths of imagination, only madness and unfathomable evils were found
    - Edgar Allen Poe was another who inverted transcendentalist promises
      - Explored the depths of humanity and came out with a blind obsession with death and violence
  - Sentimental Fiction
    - Major form of American fiction that grew out of the responses of white writers to the abuses of slavery
      - Uncle Tom's Cabin (1851)
    - Aimed to rouse pity for the oppressed + offered a natural form for novelists writing about evils of slavery
    - Some shifted the focus to gender - dangers faced by young women - driven to compromise morals from poverty + family loss
      - The WIde, Wide World (1850)

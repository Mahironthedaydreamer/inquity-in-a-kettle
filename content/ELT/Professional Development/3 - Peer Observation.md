## I. Nature of peer observation

- Usually refers to a teacher or other observer closely watching + monitoring a language lesson to understand some aspect of teaching, learning or classroom interactions
- While "observation" is associated with a coordinator/visitor coming to a classroom to carry out a supervision/evaluation for appraisal, peer observation attempts to separate evaluation from observation - and link it to part of the professional development process.

## II. Purpose and benefits of peer observation

- For observers:
  - Provides opportunities for novice teachers to see how an experienced teacher handles a class
  - New viewpoints on how to handle a problem from observing how one's peers handled it - may also trigger reflections about one's own teaching
- For the observed teacher:
  - The observer can provide "objective" views of the lesson + collect information about the lesson not seen by the teacher
- For both:
  - A chance to share ideas and expertise, as well as a chance to discuss problems and concerns
  - Provides a chance to see how other teachers teach and receive feedback
  - A method to collect information about teaching and classroom processes
  - A good way to develop self-awareness of one's own teaching

## III. Scope and Limitations

- Can only observe things that are visible, such as:
  - Timing: How much time is spent for each activity
  - Activities: The types of activities the teacher uses during the lesson
  - Questioning techniques: The types of questions the teachers ask
  - Participation: Which learners actively participate in the lesson
  - Classroom language: The kind of language learners produce
- Other important aspects that are NOT observable:
  - Decision making: The kinds of decision considered during the lesson
  - Engagement: The extent to which learners find aspects of the lesson interesting and engaging
  - Problems: Difficulties the teacher experiences during the lesson that aren't visible to an observer
  - Teaching principles: The principles and methodologies that inform the teacher's approach to the lesson
- Thus, peer observation involves discussions and reflections in order to arrive at an understanding of the meaning of the events observed.
- Requires careful planning and implementation
  - Lessons are dynamic if not unpredictable events with many different participants and several different things can happen simultaneously
  - Therefore, specific procedures are needed.

## IV. Procedures used for peer observation

### 1. Written narrative

- A narrative account of the lesson as a whole
- Written by the observer instead of the teacher
- An account of the main structure + development of the lesson, the activities the teacher employed and the significant time periods within  the lesson.
- Important not to describe _everything_ in the lesson - should be objective and precise.
- Advantages and disadvantages
  - Advantages: Can be useful in helping to see what the lesson structure was like + how the teacher implemented/departed from the lesson plan.
  - Disadvantages: Many aspects are difficult to describe accurately in real time, such as the actual language that was used during a teacher-student exchange.

### 2. Field Notes

- Consist of brief descriptions in note form of key events that occurred throughout the lesson, including interpretations of relevant incidents.
- Sometimes time-based, or may be linked to key activities that occurred during the lesson
- Advantages and disadvantages:
  - Advantages: Flexible way to observe a lesson, only relevant information is included (mostly)
  - Disadvantages: Collected information may be insufficient

### 3. Checklists

- Structured inventory listing features of a lesson that the observer completes as he/she observes the lesson
- Advantages and disadvantages:
  - Advantages: Highly focused and easy to complete, provides a systematic way to collect information on specific aspects of a lesson
  - Disadvantages: Some aspects are difficult to identify, sometimes focuses on trivial aspects of the lesson and fail to account for much of what happens

## V. Focus of an observation

- **Typically:** _How_ a lesson begins or ends, _how_ much time does the lesson take, which _tasks_ are assigned and _how_ they are handed out, _how_ misbehaving students are dealt with, _how_ learning groups are organized, _how_ students are supervised during the lesson, _how_ questions are asked,...
- **Other notable topics:**
  - **Teacher time management**: Allotment of time to different activities
  - **Student performance:** Language use, procedures, and interaction patterns
  - **Time on task:** The extent to which students were engaged
  - **Teacher's action zone:** The extent the teacher interacted with some students more frequently than others
  - **Use of the textbook:**  How much was the textbook used and the extracted contents from it
  - **Pair - Group work:** How students  complete a task, the language they used, student time on said task and the dynamics of group activities

## VI. Implementing peer observation

- Choose colleague(s) to work with
  - Someone who's teaching the same course/textbook, or can simply be mutual interest
- Each teacher takes turns at teaching and observing, with proper procedures as follows:
  - Arrange for a pre-observation orientation session
  - Decide on observation procedures to be used
  - Complete observation using procedures that were agreed on
  - Arrange a post-observation session

### Supporting teachers in implementing peer observation:

- Administrators and supervisors' role to play, and can support through:
  - Survey teachers to find out the support they need
  - Gather resources on classroom observation, or invite outside experts to give workshops on how to do observations
  - Ask teachers for explanation
  - Free up time for engagement
  - Encourage to speak up, report findings to other peers
